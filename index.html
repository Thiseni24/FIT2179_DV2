Act One

Where Cinema Comes From

Cinema is a global art form, but the map reveals an uneven landscape. A handful of countries dominate global film production, while dozens of others contribute smaller but culturally vital industries. The story of where films come from is also a story of where the industry concentrates its resources.

The United States, India, France, Japan and the United Kingdom dominate global film output, but film production reaches nearly every continent.

Global film production grew steadily for five decades before collapsing during the COVID-19 pandemic — a sharp reminder of how fragile creative industries can be.

Act Two

How Cinema Changed Over Time

Cinema doesn't sit still. Genres rise and fall in waves, languages compete for screen time, and audience tastes shape what gets made. These two charts trace the shifting tides of global cinema across five decades.

Drama and Comedy have always anchored cinema, but the rise of Action in the 80s, Horror's recurring waves, and Animation's steady growth tell their own stories.

English has held the #1 spot in every decade since 1970, but the languages competing for the remaining places have reshuffled dramatically — Italian declined, French stayed steady, and Korean climbed into the top 10 in the post-Parasite era.

Act Three

The Industry Behind the Films

Films are made by industries — and industries have structure, money and power dynamics. A handful of studios produce most of the commercial output. Bigger budgets don't guarantee bigger returns. And different audience communities surprisingly agree on what's worth watching.

The relationship between film volume and revenue is far from linear. Studios such as Disney generate exceptionally high revenue because they own major franchises like Marvel, Star Wars, Pixar, and live-action remakes, which already have established global fan bases. These franchises reduce financial risk, attract stronger investor confidence, receive larger marketing budgets, and secure wider international distribution. As a result, a small number of blockbuster releases can generate billions in revenue, allowing franchise-driven studios to outperform competitors with much larger catalogues of standalone films.

Most films don't recover their production budgets. The break-even diagonal separates the hits from the misses, and many films sit well below it.

A film's IMDb rating and the number of reviews it receives don't always move together. Some highly-rated films are barely discussed; some heavily-discussed films are mid-rated. Quality and conversation aren't the same thing.

Although IMDb and TMDB attract different communities, both platforms show remarkably similar opinions across decades. Older films consistently receive higher ratings, suggesting that cultural longevity, critical recognition, and lasting influence shape audience perception more strongly than platform differences. TMDB ratings remain slightly higher across every decade, which may reflect differences in platform behaviour: TMDB is often used by dedicated film enthusiasts who actively catalogue and track films they choose to watch, while IMDb reaches a broader mainstream audience and larger voting base. Larger audiences typically introduce a wider range of opinions, causing ratings to become more moderate over time.

Act Four

The Anatomy of a Film

Behind every box-office number is a creative choice. Each genre has its own distinctive shape — its typical runtime, rating, scale. Genres mix in specific ways. And the certification a film receives shapes its commercial life. These charts pull back the curtain on what makes a film what it is.

Each genre traces a distinct shape — Animation runs short and well-rated, Drama runs long and high-prestige, Horror is short and rough. The shape is the fingerprint.

Genres don't exist in isolation. Drama + Comedy, Drama + Romance, Action + Adventure — the strongest ribbons show how multi-genre films are made.

PG-13 has become Hollywood's commercial sweet spot — more inclusive than R, more grown-up than PG, and the certification most blockbusters target.

So, what does the world on screen look like?

Global cinema is shaped by concentration and diversity at the same time. A few countries and a few languages dominate, while genres and tastes continue to evolve. Behind every film is an industry of power and risk — and yet each film carries its own creative fingerprint. Both stories are true at once.
