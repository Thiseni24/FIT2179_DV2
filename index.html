Act One

Where Cinema Comes From

Behind every film is a place. While cinema reaches audiences worldwide, production remains concentrated in a small number of powerful filmmaking hubs.

Film production is heavily concentrated in a relatively small number of countries, with the United States, India, China, and several major European industries accounting for a large share of global output. The map highlights a noticeable shift toward Asia, where rapidly expanding film industries have become major contributors to worldwide cinema production. In contrast, many countries across Africa remain underrepresented, not because of a lack of storytelling traditions, but because limited investment, smaller domestic film markets, lower access to filmmaking technology and weaker global distribution networks make large-scale film production more difficult. Rather than being evenly distributed across the world, modern cinema is shaped by a handful of highly productive regional hubs that exert significant cultural and economic influence over the global film industry.

Act Two

The Evolution of the Silver Screen

Cinema is constantly reinventing itself. Genres surge and fade, languages rise to prominence, and changing audience tastes reshape what reaches the screen.

Global film production expanded steadily for decades, but the most dramatic growth occurred after the early 2000s as digital filmmaking lowered production costs and made film creation more accessible worldwide. By the late 2010s, annual releases had reached record highs, reflecting the rapid expansion of international film industries and growing demand for content across cinemas and streaming platforms. However, this long period of growth was abruptly disrupted in 2020 when the COVID-19 pandemic halted productions, delayed releases, and closed cinemas around the world. Although production has since recovered, the industry has not fully returned to its pre-pandemic trajectory. Shifts toward streaming-first releases, rising production costs, labour strikes, and increased financial caution from studios have slowed the pace of recovery. Together, these trends suggest that the pandemic did not simply interrupt global cinema, it accelerated long-term changes that continue to reshape how films are produced and distributed today.

The 2000s and 2010s marked a turning point for global cinema. Advances in digital effects and filmmaking technology fuelled the rise of visually spectacular action and animation films, while drama and comedy remained consistently popular due to their broad appeal and ability to adapt to changing social and cultural trends. Horror experienced a renaissance during the 2010s as filmmakers increasingly used the genre to explore contemporary anxieties, social issues, and psychological themes (prominent examples of this being films such as Get Out (2017) and Midsommar (2019)) rather than relying solely on traditional scares. Although streaming platforms expanded access to a wider range of films, evolving audience tastes and new storytelling approaches played an even greater role in reshaping the genre landscape. Together, these trends reveal how global cinema evolved from a relatively stable genre mix into a more diverse and dynamic industry.

~67% of films analysed are in English

While English remained dominant through Hollywood's global distribution networks, many film industries reached peak output during the 2000s and early 2010s. Advances in digital filmmaking lowered production costs, while expanding multiplex cinemas and international distribution created greater demand for films worldwide. As streaming platforms emerged, audiences gained easier access to foreign-language content, helping regional industries such as Korean and Spanish cinema reach global viewers. At the same time, traditionally influential industries including Japanese and Italian cinema became less prominent as competition increased and audience attention spread across a wider range of international productions. Together, these trends suggest that global cinema has become increasingly decentralised, with influence shared across a broader range of regional film industries rather than concentrated in a few long-established producers.

Act Three

The Economics of Cinema

Not every blockbuster begins with a huge budget, and not every acclaimed film finds a mass audience. These charts explore how money, attention, and audience perception combine to determine success in cinema.

The relationship between film volume and revenue is far from linear. Studios such as Disney generate exceptionally high revenue because they own major franchises like Marvel, Star Wars, Pixar, and live-action remakes, which already have established global fan bases. These franchises reduce financial risk, attract stronger investor confidence, receive larger marketing budgets, and secure wider international distribution. As a result, a small number of blockbuster releases can generate billions in revenue, allowing franchise-driven studios to outperform competitors with much larger catalogues of standalone films.

While higher-budget films generally achieve higher box office revenues, the relationship is far from guaranteed. Larger budgets allow studios to invest in visual effects, major actors, global marketing campaigns, and wider theatrical releases, increasing a film's potential audience and commercial reach. However, these advantages also come with greater financial risk, as expensive productions require significantly larger returns to become profitable. The chart shows that many high-budget films still generate losses due to factors such as poor critical reception, franchise fatigue, or intense competition at release. At the same time, lower-budget films can achieve exceptional returns by attracting dedicated audiences, generating strong word-of-mouth, or benefiting from streaming and niche markets. Together, these patterns suggest that budget expands a film's opportunity for success, but audience reception, marketing effectiveness, and cultural relevance ultimately determine whether that investment pays off.

Success in cinema is not determined by ratings alone. While highly rated films often attract more audience attention, the largest box-office successes are concentrated where strong ratings and widespread discussion intersect. This suggests that commercial success is driven not only by quality, but also by a film's ability to generate conversation and cultural relevance. At the same time, the presence of highly rated yet less-discussed films highlights how critical acclaim does not always translate into financial success or mainstream visibility. Together, these patterns reveal that the most profitable films are often those that balance audience approval, broad appeal, and public attention.

Although IMDb and TMDB attract different communities, both platforms show remarkably similar opinions across decades. Older films consistently receive higher ratings, suggesting that cultural longevity, critical recognition, and lasting influence shape audience perception more strongly than platform differences. TMDB ratings remain slightly higher across every decade, which may reflect differences in platform behaviour: TMDB is often used by dedicated film enthusiasts who actively catalogue and track films they choose to watch, while IMDb reaches a broader mainstream audience and larger voting base.

Act Four

The Patterns Beneath

Every film leaves a fingerprint. Genres, certifications, and audience expectations combine in different ways to shape the stories we see on screen.

Film certifications influence more than just who can watch a movie, they are also associated with differences in audience reach, runtime, and reception. Family-oriented films (G and PG) tend to achieve the highest popularity, reflecting their ability to attract viewers across multiple age groups. In contrast, PG-13 and R-rated films are substantially longer, often allowing for more complex stories and mature themes, yet this does not necessarily translate into higher IMDb ratings.

Each genre leaves a distinctive production and audience "fingerprint," revealing that success in cinema is achieved through very different strategies. Genres such as action and animation combine large budgets with high revenues, reflecting the financial demands of visual effects, franchise building, and global distribution. In contrast, biography, drama, and romance films achieve strong ratings despite more modest production spending, suggesting that audiences often reward compelling stories and performances as much as technical spectacle.

Film genres rarely exist in isolation. The strongest connections occur between genres that complement one another, such as action and adventure, crime and thriller, or drama and romance. Drama emerges as the most interconnected genre, reflecting its ability to add emotional depth to a wide range of stories. The diagram also highlights how modern cinema increasingly relies on hybrid storytelling, where films combine multiple genres to appeal to diverse audiences and balance different forms of entertainment. Rather than being defined by a single genre, many successful films are built from combinations of themes, tones, and narrative styles, revealing the interconnected nature of contemporary filmmaking.

So, what does the world on screen look like?

Behind every point, line, and connection in this story are the choices of filmmakers and the tastes of audiences across generations. Cinema is not defined by a single country, genre, language, or formula for success. It is shaped by millions of people creating, watching, discussing, and sharing stories. The patterns revealed throughout this journey help explain how films are made and consumed, but they also highlight something equally important: the enduring human desire to tell stories and see ourselves reflected on screen.
