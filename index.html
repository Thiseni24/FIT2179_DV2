Act One

Where Cinema Comes From

Behind every film is a place. While cinema reaches audiences worldwide, production remains concentrated in a small number of powerful filmmaking hubs.

Film production is heavily concentrated in a relatively small number of countries, with the United States, India, China, and major European industries accounting for a large share of global output.

The map highlights a shift toward Asia, where rapidly expanding film industries have become major contributors to worldwide cinema. In contrast, many countries across Africa remain underrepresented due to lower investment, smaller domestic film markets, limited access to filmmaking technology, and weaker global distribution networks.

Act Two

The Evolution of the Silver Screen

Cinema is constantly reinventing itself. Genres surge and fade, languages rise to prominence, and changing audience tastes reshape what reaches the screen.

Global film production expanded steadily for decades, with the fastest growth after the early 2000s as digital filmmaking lowered production costs and made film creation more accessible worldwide.

This long period of growth was disrupted in 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic halted productions, delayed releases, and closed cinemas. The slower recovery suggests the pandemic did not simply interrupt cinema, but accelerated long-term changes in how films are produced and distributed.

The 2000s and 2010s marked a turning point for global cinema. Digital effects and filmmaking technology fuelled the rise of visually spectacular Action and Animation films, while Drama and Comedy remained popular because they adapt easily to changing social and cultural themes.

Horror experienced a renaissance during the 2010s as filmmakers used the genre to explore contemporary anxieties and social issues, rather than relying only on traditional scares. Together, these shifts show cinema moving toward a more diverse and dynamic genre landscape.

~67% of films analysed are in English

While English remained dominant through Hollywood's global distribution networks, many film industries reached peak output during the 2000s and early 2010s. Digital filmmaking lowered production costs, while multiplex expansion and international distribution increased demand for films worldwide.

Streaming platforms made foreign-language content easier to access, helping regional industries such as Korean and Spanish cinema reach global viewers. At the same time, Japanese and Italian cinema became less prominent as global attention spread across more international producers.

Act Three

The Economics of Cinema

Not every blockbuster begins with a huge budget, and not every acclaimed film finds a mass audience. These charts explore how money, attention, and audience perception combine to determine success in cinema.

The relationship between film volume and revenue is far from linear. Studios such as Disney generate exceptionally high revenue due to their ownership of major franchises such as Marvel, Star Wars, Pixar, and live-action remakes, which already have established global fan bases.

These franchises reduce financial risk, attract stronger investor confidence, receive larger marketing budgets, and secure wider international distribution. As a result, a small number of blockbuster releases can outperform much larger catalogues of standalone films.

While higher-budget films generally achieve higher box office revenues, the relationship is far from guaranteed. Larger budgets allow studios to invest in visual effects, major actors, and wider theatrical releases — but they also come with greater financial risk, as expensive productions require significantly larger returns to become profitable.

Many high-budget films still generate losses due to poor critical reception, franchise fatigue, or intense competition. Meanwhile, lower-budget films can achieve exceptional returns through dedicated audiences and strong word-of-mouth — proving that budget creates opportunity, but audience reception ultimately decides whether it pays off.

Success in cinema is not determined by ratings alone. While highly rated films often attract more audience attention, the largest box-office successes are concentrated where strong ratings and widespread discussion intersect.

This suggests that commercial success is driven not only by quality, but also by a film's ability to generate conversation and cultural relevance. The presence of highly rated yet less-discussed films shows how critical acclaim does not always translate into financial success.

Although IMDb and TMDB attract different communities, both platforms show remarkably similar opinions across decades. Older films consistently receive higher ratings, suggesting that cultural longevity and critical recognition shape audience perception more strongly than platform differences.

TMDB ratings remain slightly higher across every decade, which may reflect differences in platform behaviour: TMDB is often used by dedicated film enthusiasts who actively catalogue films, while IMDb reaches a broader mainstream audience and larger voting base.

Act Four

The Patterns Beneath

Every film leaves a fingerprint. Genres, certifications, and audience expectations combine in different ways to shape the stories we see on screen.

Film certifications influence more than just who can watch a movie — they are also associated with differences in audience reach, runtime, and reception.

Family-oriented films (G and PG) tend to achieve the highest popularity, reflecting their ability to attract viewers across multiple age groups. PG-13 and R-rated films are substantially longer, often allowing for more complex stories and mature themes, yet this does not necessarily translate into higher IMDb ratings.

Each genre leaves a distinctive "fingerprint", revealing that success in cinema is achieved through very different strategies. Genres such as Action and Animation combine large budgets with high revenues, reflecting the financial demands of visual effects and global distribution.

In contrast, Biography, Drama, and Romance films achieve strong ratings despite more modest production spending — suggesting that audiences often reward compelling stories and performances as much as technical spectacle.

Film genres rarely exist in isolation. The strongest connections occur between genres that complement one another, such as Action and Adventure, Crime and Thriller, or Drama and Romance.

Drama emerges as the most interconnected genre, reflecting its ability to add emotional depth to a wide range of stories. The diagram also highlights how modern cinema increasingly relies on hybrid storytelling, where films combine multiple genres to appeal to diverse audiences.

So, what does the world on screen look like?

Behind every point, line, and connection in this story are the choices of filmmakers and the tastes of audiences across generations. Cinema is not defined by a single country, genre, language, or formula for success — it is shaped by millions of people creating, watching, discussing, and sharing stories. The patterns revealed throughout this journey highlight something equally important: the enduring human desire to tell stories and see ourselves reflected on screen.
